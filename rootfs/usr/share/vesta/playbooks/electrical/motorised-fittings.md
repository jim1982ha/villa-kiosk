# A fitting nobody trusts is a fitting nobody uses

Motorised covers fail quietly. They do not alarm; they simply stop being
operated, and the automation controlling them gets disabled by somebody who got
tired of it. Disabled automations around motorised fittings are therefore a
symptom worth reading, not just a configuration observation.

## Check, in order

1. **Lost limits.** Most cover motors learn their travel and store it. A loss
   of that calibration shows as travel that is short, long, or refuses to
   complete, often after a power interruption or a manual override at the
   fitting. Recalibration is the fix and it is a task, not a part.
2. **Stall against obstruction.** A stall repeating at the *same* position is
   mechanical — a track, a bracket, something in the way. A stall at varying
   positions is the motor, its supply, or its coupling.
3. **Direction and duration.** Compare the time taken each way. Steadily
   lengthening travel in one direction is friction building in the track or a
   cord slipping.
4. **Battery, where the motor is battery-powered.** Cover motors draw heavily
   and are the first devices to misbehave on a falling cell — usually as
   incomplete travel rather than as a low-battery report.
5. **Look for the human workaround.** An automation disabled, a schedule
   emptied, a fitting always found in one position. That tells you the fault is
   old and has already cost the property its use.

## What you may not conclude

That an unused fitting is a broken one. Some are simply not wanted. Ask the
question in the finding rather than asserting the answer.

## What to say

Which fitting, what the travel is doing, and whether this is a recalibration, an
obstruction or a power problem. Where you found a disabled automation nearby,
say so — it is usually the oldest evidence available and it dates the fault.
