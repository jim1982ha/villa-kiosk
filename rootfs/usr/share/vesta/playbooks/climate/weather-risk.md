# The only procedure here that acts on what has not happened yet

Every other check reacts. This one anticipates, and it is the shape a rule
engine cannot take at all: a rule fires on a measurement, and there is nothing
to measure yet.

## Check, in order

1. **What is forecast, when, and with what confidence.** State the lead time —
   a warning with hours of notice justifies a different response from one with
   days.
2. **What is exposed.** Open covers and awnings, outdoor furniture, anything
   that moves in wind. Motorised fittings can usually be closed in advance,
   which is the single most useful preparatory action available.
3. **Expect a supply interruption** where storms and the electricity supply are
   correlated locally. That means: what does not restart itself, and what is
   mid-cycle. The power-events procedure lists the recovery checks; run them
   afterwards, not instead.
4. **Water ingress and drainage.** Pool overflow, drains that back up, sumps,
   and any pump that will be asked to work harder. A drainage pump that was
   already marginal fails during the event, not before it.
5. **Occupancy.** Preparation advice for an empty property is a task list for
   the facility manager; for an occupied one it is also information the guests
   need. The audience changes what you write.

## What you may not conclude

That a forecast is an event. Say what is forecast and what preparation is
proportionate. Repeated warnings for weather that is normal for the season are
the fastest way to be ignored — in a climate with a wet season, rain is the
default state and not a finding.

## What to say

The event, the lead time, and a short ordered list of preparations that fit that
lead time. Afterwards, close the loop: say what the event actually did, because
that is what makes the next warning credible.
