# Damage comes from duration, not from peaks

A humid afternoon is weather. Humidity that stays high for days is a building
problem, and it is one of the few findings where nothing visible has gone wrong
yet and acting early is the whole value.

## Check, in order

1. **Duration above the comfortable band, not the maximum reached.** Sustained
   is the risk term; a spike after rain is normal in a humid climate.
2. **Which spaces, and are they occupied?** Closed unoccupied rooms are the
   classic case: no air movement, no cooling, contents that absorb. Wardrobes,
   store rooms and rooms shut up between lets are where damage appears first.
3. **Is ventilation running?** Extract fans and ventilation units that are off,
   on a schedule that no longer matches occupancy, or that have failed
   silently. Compare against the wet rooms they serve.
4. **Cooling as dehumidification.** Air conditioning removes moisture only while
   the compressor runs; a unit oversized for a space cools quickly, stops, and
   leaves the room cold and damp. Short cycling plus high humidity is that
   pattern.
5. **Outdoor against indoor.** If indoor tracks outdoor closely, the space is
   ventilated to outside and the fix is envelope or scheduling. If indoor stays
   high while outdoor falls, moisture is being generated or trapped inside.

## What you may not conclude

That there is mould. You are looking at conditions that favour it. Say
"conditions that support growth, sustained for this long, in these spaces" —
which is actionable and honest — and let a person look.

## What to say

Which spaces, for how long, and what is or is not ventilating them. Frame it as
a preventable cost: contents and finishes are more expensive than running a fan.
